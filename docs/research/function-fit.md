## Part B. Principles from sources (recorded as they land)

Each principle has these fields: Rule; Conditions; Trade-off; Resolution ("given X, do Z rather than Y"); Source (strength); Era (T = timeless, E = era-bound, with date). "UNCONDITIONED in the source" means that the source gives no conditions; do not use that rule as a universal rule.

### B1. The aesthetic-usability effect: what the evidence says, and its limits

**B1.1 People rate an attractive interface as easier to use, before use and often after use.**
- Rule: Kurosu and Kashimura (Hitachi Design Center, CHI '95 short paper, pp. 292-293): 252 students (156 design, 96 psychology) rated 26 cash-machine screen layouts on paper for "apparent usability" and beauty. "Relatively high correlation (0.589) was obtained between these two scales." Their conclusion: "the apparent usability is less correlated with the inherent usability", and designers "should strive not only to improve the inherent usability but also brush up the apparent usability". Tractinsky (CHI '97) repeated it in Israel and found r = 0.59 between beauty and apparent usability, with mostly negligible correlation to the inherent usability factors. Tractinsky, Katz and Ikar, "What is beautiful is usable", *Interacting with Computers* 13(2):127-145, 2000: an ATM simulation, with aesthetics and actual usability both manipulated, measured before and after use. Abstract: "Pre-experimental measures indicate strong correlations between system's perceived aesthetics and perceived usability"; after use, the aesthetic level affected perceived usability and the actual usability level did not.
- Source: Kurosu and Kashimura 1995, https://chi1995.chistatic.hosting.acm.org/proceedings/shortppr/mk_bdy.htm (read 13 Oct 2026). **Primary** (the original paper, short form). Tractinsky 1997: search-result summary only, **Strong secondary, read second-hand**. Tractinsky et al. 2000: abstract read at https://academic.oup.com/iwc/article-abstract/13/2/127/898608. **Primary (abstract only).** NN/g restates the effect: "users' tendency to perceive attractive products as more usable" (Kate Moran, "The Aesthetic-Usability Effect", page dated 3 Feb 2024, reviewed 1 Sep 2026, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/aesthetic-usability-effect/). **Primary (NN/g).**
- A detail that the popular account drops: in Kurosu and Kashimura's own table, "familiarity" correlated with apparent usability at 0.730, **more** than beauty did (0.589). So the first study already shows that a familiar layout looks usable at least as strongly as a beautiful one. This agrees with Tuch et al. 2012 (#104 W4): high prototypicality is liked within 50 ms.
- Era: **T** (a perception and judgement effect). The specific things that look "beautiful" are era-bound.

**B1.2 The effect has limits: it is a correlation of perceptions, it is strongest before use, and well-controlled experiments often do not find it.**
- Rule and evidence against the strong form:
  - Kurosu and Kashimura measured perceptions of paper printouts with no use. Tractinsky (1997) named this as a method concern, because usability is normally measured during or after use. **Primary / Strong secondary.**
  - Tuch, Roth, Hornbæk, Opwis and Bargas-Avila, "Is beautiful really usable?", *Computers in Human Behavior* 28(5):1596-1607, 2012: 80 participants used one of four versions of an online shop (aesthetics low or high, usability low or high). Abstract (via search result): "Aesthetics does not affect perceived usability. In contrast, usability has an effect on post-use perceived aesthetics"; the "what is beautiful is usable" notion "can be reversed under certain conditions"; frustration from poor usability lowered the beauty rating. **Strong secondary (peer-reviewed); abstract read through a search result, not at source.**
  - Grishin and Gillan, "Exploring the Boundary Conditions of the Effect of Aesthetics on Perceived Usability", *Journal of Usability Studies* 14(2):76-104, Feb 2019: a website in four versions (aesthetics crossed with usability), tasks on four separate occasions. The hypothesised effect of aesthetics on usability judgements did not appear; SUS scores followed manipulated usability and the occasion. **Strong secondary; abstract read through a search result.**
  - Hassenzahl and Monk, "The inference of perceived usability from beauty", *Human-Computer Interaction* 25(3), 2010: a review of about 15 studies; it found few studies that manipulated aesthetics experimentally. **Read second-hand only** (through papers that cite it).
  - Sonderegger and Sauer, *Applied Ergonomics* 41:403-410, 2010: 60 adolescents, two functionally identical phone simulations; the attractive one was rated more usable **and** gave shorter task times. Sauer and Sonderegger 2011 (*Behaviour & IT*), same design: attractive raised perceived usability but gave **longer** task times. **Strong secondary; abstracts via search result.** So the effect of beauty on actual performance is not settled.
  - NN/g: "A pretty design can make users forgiving of minor usability problems, but not of large ones." **Primary (NN/g).**
- Conditions (from the sources together): the effect is strongest (1) before use or after brief use, (2) for first impressions and judgements made from a picture, (3) when the usability faults are minor. It weakens or reverses when usability faults are strong and the person meets them in use (Tuch 2012), and over repeated use (Grishin 2019).
- Era: **T.**

**B1.3 The part of "beauty" that predicts perceived usability is mostly order and clarity, not originality.**
- Rule: Lavie and Tractinsky ("Assessing dimensions of perceived visual aesthetics of web sites", *IJHCS* 60(3):269-298, 2004) found two dimensions. "Classical" aesthetics: "These notions emphasize orderly and clear design and are closely related to many of the design rules advocated by usability experts." "Expressive" aesthetics "is manifested by the designers' creativity and originality and by the ability to break design conventions." Schrepp, Otten, Blum and Thomaschewski (*IJIMAI* 6(6):78-85, Dec 2020): in two large studies, **visual clarity** (order, alignment, low visual complexity) was a strong mediator of the link between perceived aesthetics and perceived usability.
- Source: Lavie and Tractinsky abstract via https://www.talks.cam.ac.uk/talk/index/7931/ (**Strong secondary, abstract read**). Schrepp et al.: abstract summary via search result (**Strong secondary, second-hand**).
- Consequence for the founder's question: the "beauty" that buys trust and patience is largely the same thing as good function (clear order, alignment, a familiar structure). The "beauty" that expresses a brand (originality, broken conventions) does not carry that halo, and it is the part that can pull against function.
- Era: **T** (the two-dimension model). What reads as "original" is **E**.

**B1.4 For a reviewer: beauty hides function faults from the person who judges a picture.**
- Rule (people): NN/g: "when our goal is to find problems so we can fix them, the aesthetic-usability effect can get in the way"; a participant who struggled on a task still rated the site easy and praised its colours. Advice: watch "what users do and how it relates to what they say"; ask open, non-leading questions. **Primary (NN/g).**
- Rule (a Claude critic): house research #99 (`docs/research/visual-critic-reliability.md`) V5: "Fooling the LVLM Judges" (arXiv:2505.15249, ACL 2025): visual edits that do not change content, for example added decoration, made "all tested LVLM judges" give inflated scores, and prompt-based mitigation did not remove it (**Weak**, abstract). V4: a judge that scores task accomplishment and aesthetics as separate dimensions agreed with human preference 69.5% against 48.5% for a single score. V11 (Duan et al., CHI 2024): a model critic was worst on the "aesthetic and minimalist design" heuristic and missed "global" issues.
- I found **no study that measures expert reviewers** (designers doing a heuristic review) missing more function faults on an attractive render (search: "expert evaluators biased by visual aesthetics heuristic evaluation"; "halo effect usability evaluation aesthetics": no such study in the results). So the reviewer half of the claim is an **inference** from the user evidence (B1.1, B1.2) and from the VLM-judge evidence (#99 V5). Confidence 0.7 for people; 0.75 for a VLM critic.
- Conditions: the risk is highest exactly where the house judges: (1) from a static render, with no use (B1.2 condition 1-2); (2) by the founder, who picks blind and fast (design-round §3); (3) by a VLM critic that scores a page whole.
- Trade-off: a separate function pass costs critic time and tokens; it adds one more checklist to a critic that #99 V8 says loses accuracy as rules are added.
- Resolution: given a judgement made from a render, judge function **before** beauty, and as a separate pass with its own verdict: walk the elements and ask the function questions in Part D, and only then score the whole. Given a candidate that is clearly the most beautiful, run the function walk on it first, not last. Given a function fault found on the leader, report it even when the candidate wins on looks; NN/g's "forgiving of minor ... not of large" sets the threshold: a fault that stops a task is never outweighed by beauty.
- Era: **T** for people; **E (2024-2026)** for the VLM-judge evidence.

### B2. Affordances and signifiers: what an element can do, and what it shows that it can do

**B2.1 Separate what an element can do (the affordance) from the cue that shows it (the signifier). Design the cue.**
- Rule: Gibson invented "affordance" for "a relationship: the actions possible by a specific agent on a specific environment" (Norman's restatement). Norman brought "perceived affordance" into design in 1988, and in 2008 he wrote that "the term has been widely used and misused", and: "Forget affordances: what people need, and what design must provide, are signifiers." A signifier "is some sort of indicator, some signal in the physical or social world that can be interpreted meaningfully". Signifiers are "often useful, but of mixed reliability".
- On a screen, almost everything can be clicked, so the affordance is nearly constant. What the designer controls is the signifier: underline, shape, fill, border, depth, position, cursor, label. So "does it look clickable?" is a question about signifiers.
- Conditions: a signifier works only against a convention the visitor already knows (blue or underlined link, filled rectangle button, box with an edge for a field). Conventions move with eras (B2.3).
- Source: Norman, "Signifiers, not affordances", jnd.org, 17 Nov 2008 (also *ACM Interactions* 15(6):18-19), https://jnd.org/signifiers-not-affordances/ (read 13 Oct 2026). **Primary** (the author of the design term). Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception* (1979), not read; restated by Norman.
- Era: **T.**

**B2.2 Four cases, not two: perceptible, hidden, false, and correct rejection. Hidden and false affordances cause errors.**
- Rule: Gaver ("Technology Affordances", CHI '91, pp. 79-84) separates the affordance from the perceptual information about it. "If there is no information available for an existing affordance, it is hidden and must be inferred from other evidence. If information suggests a nonexistent affordance, a false affordance exists upon which people may mistakenly try to act. Finally, people will usually not think of a given action when there is no affordance for it nor any perceptual information suggesting it" (the "correct rejection"). Abstract: "When affordances are perceptible, they offer a direct link between perception and action; hidden and false affordances lead to mistakes."
- Source: Gaver 1991, full text read (PDF course copy, lri.fr, extracted 13 Oct 2026). **Primary** (peer-reviewed conference paper).
- For every element on a render, two questions decide its cell: (1) Can a visitor act on it? (2) Does it look as if a visitor can act on it? "Yes / yes" and "no / no" pass. "Yes / no" is a **hidden** affordance (a clickable image that looks static; a whole-card link with no cue). "No / yes" is a **false** affordance (a static chip that looks like a filter chip; a non-link in the link colour; a decorative box shaped like a button).
- Era: **T.**

**B2.3 Make clickable things look clickable, and make static things look static. Flat styles need other cues.**
- Rule: NN/g (Hoa Loranger, "Beyond Blue Links: Making Clickable Elements Recognizable", 8 Mar 2015): blue is "still the safest link color"; other colours work if they stand out from body text; links in navigation and peripheral lists may not need underlines because "their location signals clickability"; buttons keep a rectangular shape, with borders or depth. Removing the 3-D effect removes one of the "strongest clickability signifiers", so flat designs need other cues. False signifiers named: "The blue headings are not clickable"; a decorative box where "The shape and label make the element look like a button when it's not"; category headings that look like buttons; static items "should not have the same color as hyperlinks". Hidden: clickable images that look like static pictures. Apply one link treatment "consistently throughout your site." Moran 2017 (NN/g, in #104 W3): weak signifiers cost about 22% more time and 25% more fixations.
- Conditions (from the sources): position can stand in for a cue (navigation, lists of links, whole-card targets); density, layout familiarity and contrast set how strong the cue must be (#104 W3: flat works when density is low, the layout is conventional, and targets have high contrast); colour cues need a check for colour-blind visitors.
- Trade-off: strong signifiers on everything make a busy page (#117 B2.1: a shadow on every card loses its value); weak signifiers make a clean page that costs time.
- Resolution: given a control inside prose or a dense mix of static and active items, give it a strong cue (underline, button shape, 3:1 edge). Given a position that already says "interactive" (a nav bar, a list of links, a whole card that is the only target in its region), a lighter cue is enough. Given a static item that sits among controls, remove every cue that the controls use (colour, shape, edge, fill), not only one.
- Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/clickable-elements/ (read 13 Oct 2026). **Primary (NN/g).** No review date shown, so the examples are from 2015.
- Era: the need for cues is **T**; the particular cues are **E** (blue underline since the 1990s, still current; 3-D buttons current as "flat 2.0"; ultra-flat 2013-2016 is dated, #104 W3).

### B3. Prominence matched to task priority

**B3.1 The most likely or most important action gets the most prominent treatment, and only one or two things get it.**
- Rule:
  - Apple HIG Buttons: "In general, use a button that has a prominent visual style for the most likely action in a view." "Keep the number of prominent buttons to one or two per view. Presenting too many prominent buttons increases cognitive load". "Use style — not size — to visually distinguish the preferred choice among multiple options." "Because of its visual prominence, people sometimes choose a primary button without reading it first", so "Don't assign the primary role to a button that performs a destructive action, even if that action is the most likely choice." (HIG `buttons.json`, read 13 Oct 2026.) **Primary.**
  - Material 3: "five button styles, in order of emphasis": elevated, filled, filled tonal, outlined, text. Filled is "the most prominent button after the FAB. It's used for final or unblocking actions in a flow". Outlined "are for actions that need attention but aren't the primary action". Text buttons "have less visual prominence, so should be used for low emphasis actions". Elevated buttons: "To prevent shadow creep, only use them when absolutely necessary, such as when the button requires visual separation from a patterned background." (material-components-android `docs/components/CommonButton.md`, read 13 Oct 2026.) **Primary.**
  - GOV.UK Design System Button: "Use a default button for the main call to action on a page." "Avoid using multiple default buttons on a single page." "Use secondary buttons for secondary calls to action". Warning buttons "Only ... for actions with serious destructive consequences". "Testing on GOV.UK has shown that using green as the colour of start buttons improved click-through rates." (https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/button/, read 13 Oct 2026.) **Primary.**
  - NN/g (Kelley Gordon, "Visual Hierarchy in UX: Definition", 17 Jan 2021): hierarchy guides the eye "in the order of intended importance"; contrast in value and saturation, scale and grouping are the tools; "If everything is contrasted, then nothing stands out." **Primary (NN/g).**
- Conditions: (1) "most likely" (Apple) and "main" (GOV.UK) are the visitor's task priority, not the business's wish; (2) the rule changes for destructive actions (Apple: prominence makes people press without reading, so the safe action may get the prominent style); (3) the count is per view, so a long page can have one per section only if each view has one (Manta's "one coral per view" agrees); (4) a patterned or photographic ground changes which treatment is prominent (Material's elevated button).
- Trade-off: brand colour is the strongest prominence tool (Apple: "Buttons that use color tend to be the most visually distinctive"). If brand expression spends that colour on grounds, art or headings, the primary action loses its unique signal.
- Resolution: given one brand accent and one primary action, reserve the accent for the action and express the brand by type, shape, illustration and a second colour. Given a page whose ground is the brand colour (a Manta turquoise hero), the action needs a colour that is not the ground (coral on turquoise) and is unique on the view. Given a destructive most-likely action, do not give it the primary style.
- Era: **T** (the principle; Apple, GOV.UK and Material agree). The style ladder (filled, tonal, outlined, text) is **E**, current since about 2021 (M3).

**B3.2 Prominence follows priority in both directions: an unimportant element must not take a top-priority treatment.**
- Rule: this is the converse of B3.1 and follows from "If everything is contrasted, then nothing stands out" (NN/g 2021) and Material's order of emphasis. impeccable `layout.md:50` says "Let hierarchy follow product priority, not framework defaults"; `colorize.md:43` says "do not spend its color on decoration". **UNCONDITIONED** in impeccable.
- Conditions: the marketing zone can give a large share of prominence to brand art (impeccable Persuade mode; Manta B1 "hero/marketing zone loud"), on the condition that the primary action stays the most prominent **interactive** thing (impeccable `mode-persuade.md:15`: "the primary action is unmistakable").
- Resolution: given brand art that is the largest object on the view, keep it non-interactive-looking and keep the action the strongest signifier of "press me". Given a decorative element that is the second most prominent thing on a working-zone view, demote it.
- Era: **T.**

### B4. One treatment, one meaning, across a site

**B4.1 Same function, same look (required). Different function, different look (strongly advised).**
- Rule:
  - WCAG 2.2 SC 3.2.4 Consistent Identification (AA): "Components that have the same functionality within a set of web pages are identified consistently." Identification "may be conveyed through visible labels, accessible names, text alternatives, icons, or other means". The converse is not required: "Components that perform different functions or produce different results are not expected to be identified consistently, even if they appear visually similar", though distinct labels are "encouraged as good design practice". (https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/consistent-identification.html, read 13 Oct 2026.) **Primary.**
  - NN/g 2015 (B2.3): "Such inconsistency causes confusion"; one link treatment "consistently throughout your site"; static items "should not have the same color as hyperlinks". Apple HIG Color (via #123 B1.6): "if you use your brand color to indicate that a borderless button is interactive, using the same or similar color to stylize noninteractive text is confusing." **Primary.**
  - GOV.UK Tag: "If you use the same tag in more than one place, make sure you keep the colour consistent." **Primary.**
  - Nielsen heuristic 4 "Consistency and Standards" (in impeccable `critique.md:456-474`, "same gesture = same behavior").
  - Gestalt similarity (design-critique §2; Wagemans et al. 2012 via #104 W4): elements that look alike are seen as one group. So a static chip that looks like a filter chip is seen as one of the filters.
- Conditions: (1) the "set of web pages" is the scope (a site, not one page); (2) a meaning can be carried by position or label, not only by colour or shape; (3) the converse (different function, different look) is a standard only where the look is the signal a visitor uses to decide to act (link colour, button shape, field edge). Two static elements with different content functions (a facts line and a flavour line) may share a look.
- Trade-off: a strict one-look-one-meaning vocabulary limits brand expression and visual variety. A brand with one signature colour wants to use it widely.
- Resolution: given a treatment that signals interaction (link colour, underline, button shape, control edge, depth, the "tappable" fill), reserve it for interactive elements in every zone of the site. Given a treatment that only signals brand or grouping (ground colour, illustration, display type), it may vary by zone, because it promises no action. Given a brand colour that is also the link or action colour, use a different hue or a text-safe dark partner for brand text, never the link colour (Manta night pattern; #123 B1.6).
- Source strength: Primary (WCAG, Apple, GOV.UK, NN/g).
- Era: **T.**

### B5. Decoration that mimics a control, and function dressed as decoration

**B5.1 A static element that copies a control's signifiers is a false affordance. Users click it, and lose trust in the cue.**
- Rule:
  - GOV.UK Tag: "Do not make a tag interactive by making it into a link or button." Users "are unlikely to identify the tags as something they're meant to interact with" and "would see no visual difference between interactive and non-interactive tags". Research by several teams found that some users "perceived these as buttons and tried to click on them". Name tags with adjectives, not verbs, so users do not think a click does something. **Primary** (component guidance with user-research notes).
  - Material 3 Chips: "Chips help people enter information, make selections, filter content, or trigger actions" (assist, filter, input, suggestion). Every M3 chip type is interactive; status is a badge (Communication components), not a chip. (material-components-android `docs/components/Chip.md`; Android developer "Material overview", read 13 Oct 2026.) **Primary.**
  - NN/g 2015 (B2.3): a decorative box whose "shape and label make the element look like a button when it's not"; blue non-clickable headings.
- Conditions: the risk grows with (1) the similarity of the static element to a control that is on the same page (Gestalt similarity), (2) the density of real controls nearby (a filter area), (3) a verb label. A static pill far from any control and with a noun label carries less risk.
- Trade-off: pills, chips and tags are a popular decorative device for facts and features ("Good for: surfing"), and they scan well. Making them plain text loses scannability.
- Resolution: given a static fact set near selectable chips, change at least two of the control's signifiers: no fill that the site uses for "tappable", no edge that controls use, a different radius or no container, a different text colour, a noun label; or set the facts as an inline list with separators. Given a static tag that marks status (GOV.UK sense), use a tinted fill with a text partner and no border, and never the selectable chip's shape and fill together.
- Era: **T** (the mechanism); chips as an interface device are **E** (Material, 2014 onward).

**B5.2 The converse: a functional element that is styled like decoration or like an ad is ignored.**
- Rule: NN/g (Kara Pernice, "Banner Blindness Revisited", 22 Apr 2018): banner blindness is "people's tendency to ignore page elements that they perceive (correctly or incorrectly) to be ads". "Legitimate content elements that have certain ad-like characteristics are ignored, too": ad positions (top banner, right rail), "Ad-like visual treatment, such as animation", and a promotion that "looked different from the text and the photography on the site". Advice: "Do not make content look like ads." NN/g "Photos as Web Content" (#104 W6): decorative photos are ignored. **Primary (NN/g).**
- Conditions: the risk grows with (1) a position where ads or decoration usually sit, (2) motion or a style that differs from the site's content style, (3) large, image-like blocks.
- Trade-off: brand art and distinctive promotional panels are where brand expression lives; making them look like content reduces their brand effect.
- Resolution: given an element that carries a task (a search box, a price, a "how we rank" link), never give it the styling of the site's decoration or of an ad (illustration frame, animated banner, a separate colour world). Given brand art, keep it free of required content: no instruction, price or action exists only inside the art (design-round §6 already says "Keep copy, controls and data as live UI, never baked into an image").
- Era: **T** (attention filtering). The specific "ad look" is **E**.

### B6. Brand expression and aesthetic distinctiveness against function clarity: the trade-off and its resolution

**B6.1 Expression helps function when it is spent on the element that matters most; it hurts when it breaks a basic interaction convention.**
- Rule: Google's Material 3 Expressive research ("Expressive Design: Google's UX Research", design.google, no author or date on the page; M3 Expressive launched May 2025): "46 separate research studies" with "more than 18,000 participants". In eye-tracking tests across 10 apps, "Participants were able to spot key UI elements up to four times faster in the M3 Expressive designs." The expressive tools were colour, shape, size, motion and containment; in the email example the Send button became larger, sat above the keyboard and took a secondary colour. Against this: a playlist redesign that replaced the familiar song list with loosely arranged album art looked modern, but usability scores suffered; removing text labels from email actions reduced usability. Conclusion: "When basic interaction paradigms are broken, expressive design can lead to poor usability or negative sentiment." It is "not a one-size-fits-all solution": banking-style interfaces must respect established patterns, and low familiarity reduced preference.
- Source: https://design.google/library/expressive-material-design-google-research (read 13 Oct 2026). **Primary** (first-party research summary; the studies themselves are not published, so method detail is missing). Press figures agree (Dezeen, 28 May 2025; Fast Company), **Weak**.
- Conditions: (1) expression is placed on the primary action or the key information, so it raises prominence in the order of priority (B3); (2) the interaction convention stays (a list stays a list; an action keeps its label); (3) the domain's tolerance: high-stakes or regulated tasks (banking) keep established patterns.
- Era: **E** (2025 onward as Google's platform direction); the mechanism (emphasis on the priority element speeds search) is **T**.

**B6.2 People forgive a beautiful design for small faults in a relaxed task, not in a stressed one.**
- Rule: Norman, "Emotion and design: Attractive things work better", *Interactions* ix(4):36-42, 2002: "Positive affect makes people more tolerant of minor difficulties and more flexible and creative in finding solutions." "negative affect focuses cognition". "it is essential that products designed for use under stress follow good human-centered design, for stress makes people less able to cope with difficulties". "Products designed for more relaxed, pleasant occasions can enhance their usability through pleasant, aesthetic design." NN/g agrees: beauty buys forgiveness for "minor usability problems, but not of large ones" (B1.2).
- Source: abstract at https://jnd.org/emotion-design-attractive-things-work-better/ (read 13 Oct 2026). **Primary** (the author; a magazine essay, not an experiment).
- Conditions: the stakes and stress of the task. Browsing for inspiration is relaxed; paying, booking, leaving the site for a third party, fixing an error and reading a price are stressed or high-stakes.
- Resolution: given a relaxed, browse or inspire moment (a guide page, a home hero), more expression is safe and beauty can carry small frictions. Given a high-stakes moment (price, availability, the handover to book, errors, sign-in), function clarity wins every conflict, and expression is limited to type, colour role and voice that do not move a control's signifiers. This is the same split as Manta's voice register (brand sheet §11: wit in low-stakes surfaces, "Never witty: errors, money, safety, cancellations") and impeccable's visitor modes (Persuade and Experience against Operate and Read).
- Era: **T.**

**B6.3 Distinctive in brand assets, conventional in interaction structure.**
- Rule (assembled from sources; no one source states it in these words):
  - The "beauty" that raises perceived usability is classical (orderly, clear) and familiar (Kurosu and Kashimura: familiarity 0.730 against beauty 0.589; Lavie and Tractinsky 2004; Schrepp et al. 2020; Tuch et al. 2012 prototypicality, #104 W4). "Expressive" aesthetics come from "the ability to break design conventions" (Lavie and Tractinsky) and carry no usability halo.
  - Google M3 Expressive (B6.1): expression helps when it emphasises the priority element and hurts when it breaks an interaction paradigm.
  - impeccable `mode-persuade.md:15`: "The world carries the page's parts and never absorbs them ... navigation set into a facade reads as links ... an action on a door plaque as plainly clickable ... the primary action is unmistakable". `mode-operate.md` (via #104 W4): "The world lends four things and only these: type, palette, density, and one signature move".
  - Romaniuk's distinctive-asset method (#104 W13): distinctiveness lives in assets (colour, mark, character, type, sound).
- Conditions: zone (marketing against working area), stakes (B6.2), and density (a dense list tolerates less novelty).
- Trade-off: a fully conventional structure risks the "AI look" sameness that the House DNA bans and that #102 found the founder rejects as taste; a fully expressive structure costs first-impression trust (#104 W4) and usability (B6.1).
- Resolution: given a choice of where to be different, be different in the brand layer (ground colour, illustration, display type, voice, motion character, one signature move) and conventional in the signifier layer (what a link, a button, a field, a selected state and a status look like) and in the interaction structure (lists stay lists, actions keep labels, navigation stays where visitors look for it). Given a brand idea that needs a control to look unusual, keep at least the control's core signifier (a label and a button or field shape; an underline for a link) and test that it still reads at a glance. Given a brand colour that is also a signifier colour, give the signifier meaning priority (Manta: coral reserved for the CTA; links on ink in white plus underline, never turquoise).
- Source strength: the parts are Primary to Strong secondary; the assembly is my synthesis. Confidence 0.8.
- Era: **T** as a structure; what counts as "conventional" is **E** and moves with the market.

### B7. A working taxonomy of element functions, checked against sources

**B7.1 The three public taxonomies agree on five functions; #118's set needs two changes and one addition.**

Sources (each read 13 Oct 2026; **Primary**):
- WAI-ARIA 1.2 §5.3 role categories (https://www.w3.org/TR/wai-aria-1.2/): **widget** roles "act as standalone user interface widgets" (button, link, checkbox, radio, textbox, searchbox, switch, slider, tab, option, and others); **landmark** roles are "regions of the page intended as navigational landmarks" (banner, navigation, main, search, contentinfo and others); **live region** roles (alert, log, marquee, status, timer; `status` is "advisory information for the user but is not important enough to justify an alert"); **document structure** roles "organize content in a page" and "are not usually interactive" (heading, article, img, list and others); `presentation` / `none`: "An element whose implicit native role semantics will not be mapped to the accessibility API" (decoration). `button`: "An input that allows for user-triggered actions when clicked or pressed." `link`: "An interactive reference to an internal or external resource".

### B8. One target per surface: nested controls inside a clickable card

**B8.1 A surface that is one link must not hold other controls, or each part must show which target it is.**
- Rule: HTML Living Standard, the `a` element content model: "there must be no interactive content descendant, a element descendant, or descendant with the tabindex attribute specified" (https://html.spec.whatwg.org/multipage/text-level-semantics.html#the-a-element, read 13 Oct 2026, **Primary**). NN/g 2015 (B2.3): "Make all associated elements, such as an image and its caption, clickable" together; users click outside the real target and "face major disappointment" (**Primary**). Gaver's nested affordances (B2.2): a complex control is a group of affordances "nested in space", and each must be perceptible.
- Conditions: a card with one destination can be one large target (a whole-card link with a visible cue). A card with more than one action (open, compare, next photo, view beds) has more than one affordance in one region; each needs its own signifier and its own hit area, and the "open" target must not cover them.
- Trade-off: a whole-card link is the largest, easiest target (Fitts) and needs the fewest signifiers. In-card controls add function (compare, browse photos) but make the card's surface ambiguous: a tap on the photo can mean "next photo" or "open the hostel".
- Resolution: given a card with one destination, make the whole card the target and show one cue (a link-coloured label with a chevron, or a hover state). Given in-card controls, make the title (or one explicit button) the link, give each control a distinct, standard signifier (a checkbox with a ≥3:1 edge; carousel arrows or dots with a visible control style on a scrim), and keep the controls out of the area that a visitor reads as "open this". Never put two different actions on the same visual surface (photo tap = next photo, and photo tap = open).
- Era: **T** (HTML content model; target clarity).

4. **Manta rule B16 "warm = act, cool = read" holds only if links count as "read".** Links are cool (`lagoon-text`) and they are interactive (N). The rule needs that condition stated: warm marks the action (A); navigation (N) takes the cool link signal plus an underline.

## Part D. The critic's check: one question set per element, from a render

**D1. The questions.** Each comes from a source. Ask them for each element part (B7.1 condition), in this order, **before** the critic scores beauty (B1.4 resolution).

| # | Question to the render | Source | What a "no" means |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 | What is this part's function? Code it N, A, I, S, T, R (or R-trust), C or D. | B7.1 (ARIA, Material 3, HIG) | A part with no clear function and no D code is noise (impeccable `quieter.md`, House DNA). |
| Q2 | Can a visitor act on it? Does it look as if they can? Put it in Gaver's 2x2: perceptible, hidden, false, or correct rejection. | Gaver CHI'91 (B2.2) | Hidden or false = a fault. |
| Q3 | Will the visitor know that the action they want is available here? Will they connect this control with that action? | Cognitive walkthrough Q2 and Q3 (Wharton, Rieman, Lewis and Polson 1994; Strong secondary, via a summary; book not read) | The signifier or the label fails. |
| Q4 | Which properties carry its function? Does it use any property that B7.2 reserves for **another** function? | B7.2 table (Apple HIG Color, NN/g 2015, GOV.UK Tag, Manta sheet) | Borrowing = a fault, even if the part is beautiful. |
| Q5 | Does the same look mean the same function everywhere on the page and the site? (Sweep: list each distinct treatment; each must map to one function.) | WCAG 3.2.4; NN/g 2015; Nielsen heuristic 4; GOV.UK Tag (B4) | One look, two functions = a fault. |
| Q6 | Rank the parts by visual weight (squint test). Rank them by task priority for this page. Do the two orders match, and is there one primary action? | Apple HIG Buttons, GOV.UK Button, NN/g 2021 (B3) | A low-priority part out-weighs a high one, or two parts compete as primary. |
| Q7 | Does decoration or brand sit where the visitor works, or does a functional part look like an ad or a promotion? | NN/g banner blindness 2018; mantahostels#3057 (B5.2) | Decoration takes attention or function is ignored. |
| Q8 | On a clickable surface: does each tap target have one action and its own signifier? | HTML `a` content model; NN/g 2015 (B8) | Two actions on one surface = a fault. |
| Q9 | Is the moment relaxed or high-stakes (booking, payment, error)? If high-stakes, are all signifiers conventional? | Norman 2002; M3 Expressive research (B6) | Brand experiment on a high-stakes control = a fault. |

**D2. What a render can answer, and what it cannot.**
- A render answers Q1, Q2 (only the "looks actable" half), Q4, Q5, Q6, Q7 and Q9. These are look-only questions.
- A render cannot answer "can a visitor act on it" (the other half of Q2), hover and focus states, hit areas, or nesting (Q8). These need the DOM or the code: the element's role and `href`, the `:hover` and `:focus-visible` rules, and the target size. #99 found that a vision model judges geometry poorly; it said to take geometry from code (#99, read in part). So: the critic takes the role of each part from the DOM (one script), then does Q2 by comparison: role says actable, look says not actable = hidden; role says static, look says actable = false.
- Confidence that a vision model answers Q4 and Q5 reliably: unknown. No study found that tests a model on false affordances. #99 V11 found models worst on the aesthetic heuristic, and B1.4 gives the beauty-bias risk. Treat a model's "no fault" on Q4-Q5 as unverified until a seeded test (a static pill that copies a button) shows it catches the fault.

**D3. Resolution for the round.** The function walk is a separate pass with its own output (one row per part: code, 2x2 cell, borrowed property, priority rank). The critic runs it on the candidate it likes most first (B1.4). A false or hidden affordance, a borrowed property, or a primary-action conflict that stops a task blocks a pick in the same way as a contrast failure; beauty cannot buy it back (B1.2: users forgive minor faults, not large ones; B1.4). A minor borrowing is reported, not blocking.

## Answer

1. **Every visual choice follows from function when each element part has a function code and only uses the properties that carry that function.** The code set, checked against ARIA 1.2, Material 3 and Apple HIG, is N navigate, A act, I text input, S select or filter, T status, R read (with R-trust for proof), C contain, D decorate. Changes to #118: split I into I and S; move trust from T to R-trust; add C (B7.1). The B7.2 table gives, per function, the carriers and the properties that no other function may borrow.
2. **When looks and function pull apart, visitors act on the look.** A false affordance (a static thing that looks actable) gets taps and confusion (GOV.UK Tag research; NN/g 2015). A hidden affordance (a control that does not look actable) is missed (Gaver 1991; NN/g 2015 on flat design). Function dressed as decoration or ads is ignored (NN/g banner blindness).
3. **The aesthetic-usability effect is real but conditioned.** Beauty raises *perceived* usability before use (Kurosu and Kashimura 1995, r 0.59; Tractinsky 2000), and NN/g says users forgive minor faults, not large ones. After real use, usability can change perceived beauty instead (Tuch 2012), and over time the effect can go (Grishin 2019). For a reviewer the risk is direct: a beautiful candidate hides its faults from the founder and from a model judge (#99 V5). Resolution: a function walk before beauty, run first on the favourite (D3).
4. **Brand against function** resolves by layer: distinctive in ground, illustration, display type, voice and motion; conventional in signifiers and interaction structure, most of all at high-stakes moments (Norman 2002; M3 Expressive research). Synthesis, confidence 0.8.
5. **Adopt before build:** almost every principle is stated by NN/g, Apple HIG, GOV.UK, WCAG, ARIA or HTML. impeccable states parts of B2-B5 without sources and does not state the aesthetic-usability effect, a false-affordance rule, or an element walk. The house only needs to WRITE the critic's function walk (D1-D3), the beauty-bias guard and the brand-layer line (Part E).
6. **On Manta**, the largest pull-aparts are one pill for buttons, filter chips and static facts (C1), the "white = tappable" rule broken by static white containers (C2), and a listing card with several controls inside one link (C3). Colour already has function rules; shape, fill and structure do not. Most faults are in the spec and corpus, not yet built.

Unconditioned principles found (marked as the binding standard asks): impeccable `colorize.md:43` and `layout.md:50` state prominence and colour meaning with no conditions; Manta "white = elevated/tappable, always" has no condition and its corpus breaks it; Manta B16 "warm = act, cool = read" needs the condition that links (N) are cool but interactive.

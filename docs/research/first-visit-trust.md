# What a first-time visitor to a solo advisor's site must see (#144)

Research for #144 (map #96), 14 Oct 2026. First case: mmminfra.com (mmm-infra), Mark Moseley, a solo infrastructure advisor. Written in ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English. "F1"–"F12" point to the evidence log below.

## 2. Each item

**1. A real person and a face.** Evidence: F9 (NN/g 2010 eyetracking: portraits read longer than bios; stock people ignored), F1 (NN/g 2019: "seeing a physical location and humans behind the product ... bolsters their trust"), Stanford 2002. Cyr et al. 2009 found the trust effect is indirect, through appeal and social presence. Trade-off: a photo shows that a practice is one person. mmm: "Mark is the brand" (PRODUCT.md) settles it. Use a real photo. The photo choice is in mmm-infra#8.

**2. What, for whom, where.** Evidence: F1 (NN/g 2019: hard-to-find "what the company does and whom to contact" was a main dissatisfaction). The house already tests this (design-critique §2, the 5-second test).

**3. Qualifications as evidence of work.** Evidence: F3, F4, F5 (World Bank and ADB rules: an individual is chosen on "relevant qualifications and experience"; ADB judges a CV "as per the TOR", by years in similar projects and in the similar region; a borrower's CV form asks for rows of period, organisation and title, country, and "Summary of activities performed relevant to the Assignment. Be specific."). Stanford 2002: "Be sure to give their credentials". Conditions: always for an advisor. For a trade or a consumer practice, the same job is done by a licence number and photos of finished work (Stanford: "Highlight the expertise"). Trade-off: specifics can expose a confidential mandate. mmm: see §3 for the page shape.

**4. Publications and talks.** Evidence: F6 (Edelman–LinkedIn 2024: about three-quarters of decision-makers trust thought leadership more than marketing material to judge capability; secondary), F10 (NN/g 2020: a PDF needs an HTML gateway page). Era-bound: the thought-leadership figures are 2024–2025 surveys. Conditions: when the practice sells judgement (advisors, lawyers, consultants); not needed for a plumber. mmm: keep all 24 items. Give each a page with title, date, venue and a two-line summary, then the download. Show the newest one or two on the home page.

**5. Fees or "how I am paid".** Evidence: F1 (NN/g 2016, 2019: hidden prices look "dubious"; NN/g 2006: for custom work, "list representative cases and their prices"), F8 (UK Provision of Services Regulations 2009: show a price "where the provider predetermines it"; for a bespoke price, give on request "the method for calculating the price ... or a sufficiently detailed estimate"). Conditions: show a price for a packaged or standard service (a trade call-out fee, a fixed-fee review). Do not show a rate for bespoke advisory work. Instead, say how the work is engaged and priced. Trade-off: a public day rate anchors a negotiation that MDB rules already control (ADB: "Agreement on Remuneration"; a common problem: "Consultant can't substantiate the rates", F4). mmm: no rate. One plain line on the forms of engagement is optional, for example individual contracts with banks and governments, or as a key expert through a firm (World Bank 7.33–7.35 name these routes). The voice guide bans a named client or a mandate in his voice, not a fee line, so Mark decides (mmm-infra#10).

**6. Location.** Evidence: F1 (NN/g 2019: a physical location bolsters trust), Stanford 2002 ("listing a physical address"), F5 (the CV form asks country of residence and "Working experience in the region"), F8 (UK law: a "geographic address" where it applies). Conditions: a local trade or a consumer practice shows the full address and the area served. An international advisor shows the base country and the regions of work. Trade-off: a home address on a public page. mmm: base country and regions on the profile; the legal address per item 10.

**7. Contact route.** Evidence: F1 (NN/g 2016: "prominently displaying contact information"; NN/g 2019: "At a minimum, include an email address and phone number"), F12 (GOV.UK style guide, current: "Write email addresses in full, in lower case and as active links. Do not include any other words in the link text."). Conditions: always. Phone: when the owner answers it; label it "Telephone:". Booking link: no source found; for a senior advisor it is a house judgement, not evidence. mmm: the email address as its own link text in the footer of every page and at the end of the profile. A plain "Contact" label in the navigation. No closing "Get in Touch" sentence (§4).

**8. An enquiry form and its fields.** Evidence: F7. NN/g 2016: "Keep it short"; at most "1 or 2 optional fields, and clearly label them as optional". GOV.UK question protocol: ask a question only where "you need the information to deliver the service". NN/g 2019: give the reason for each request. Field-count A/B tests point both ways (Marketo: 5 fields beat 9; Aagaard: 9 to 6 fields lost 14.23% of leads) and are weak evidence. Baymard's 8 fields is for checkout and does not transfer. Conditions: a form earns its place when the reply needs facts that people forget to give in an email (a trade's postcode and job type; a clinic's preferred times), or when the owner does not want a public email address. Default: name, email, message required. Each other field must name the action its answer changes before the reply. At most two optional fields, labelled. The "3 + 2" budget is a house judgement on NN/g's heuristic, not a measured number. mmm: no form. Email suits MDB staff, Angélique has nothing to maintain, and there is no data to protect.

**9. Privacy note at the form.** Evidence: F8 (ICO, UK GDPR Art. 13: privacy information is due "at the time when personal data are obtained"; a notice on the website "in case people happen to look there, is not enough"). Conditions: every form that collects personal data in the UK or EU. mmm: not needed while contact is by email. Whether analytics need a cookie or privacy notice was not researched.

**10. Legal identity.** Evidence: F8 (Provision of Services Regulations 2009 reg. 8; E-Commerce Regulations 2002 reg. 6: name, legal form, geographic address, register and VAT numbers, professional body and title when regulated). Conditions: a UK provider; other countries have their own rules (not researched). These are legal facts, not credentials: put them in the footer or on a small legal page, not in the profile. mmm: ask Mark his legal form, VAT status and whether he practises in a regulated profession (mmm-infra#10). The UK company-name disclosure rules for a limited company were not read.

**11. Reading level.** Evidence: F2. GOV.UK told writers to "write on GOV.UK for a 9 year old reading age" (page updated 13 Nov 2024). The current page keeps "split up sentences that are over 25 words long" and the 80% preference for plain English, which grows with expertise, but its text no longer states the age. Era: timeless as a principle; the age-9 wording is GOV.UK's until at least 2024. Conditions: task text (navigation, headings, contact, CV rows, gateway summaries) always meets it. Argument by a specialist for specialists may run longer, but technical terms are explained once. Short copy follows his slide-box model of 10–25 words (voice guide), which is already inside GOV.UK's 25. Articles keep his voice. Readers at MDBs often read English as a second language, which supports plain task text (an inference; GOV.UK names "people abroad" as readers). No formula score (Flesch and similar) was found as a sourced target for specialists.

**12. Identity on every page.** Evidence: F9 (NN/g 2002: the name or logo and a one-click link home on "every single page"). mmm: a LinkedIn post links to an article, so the article page is the first page. Each article page carries a byline, one line on who Mark is, and links to the profile and the CV.

**13. Current dates.** Evidence: Stanford 2002 ("show it's been reviewed recently"), NN/g 2016 ("comprehensive, correct and current"). mmm: the live site says "©2020" and its CV file is dated 28 May 2023 (site audit). Show the CV's date; ask Mark for a current CV (for mmm-infra#10, which does not yet ask it).

**14. Third-party proof.** Evidence: NN/g 2016's fourth factor, "connected to the rest of the web"; NN/g 2019: testimonial readers check the author's job title. Conditions: only real proof, with consent. mmm: none is held and none may be invented. The honest substitute is outbound links to his work where others host it (an ADB brief, a WAPPP article, a FIDIC task group), which shows that named bodies chose his work. Named clients stay off unless Mark clears them (voice guide rule 8).

## 3. The visitor who arrives from LinkedIn and wants to feel the CV

**What the site adds beyond the profile.** A LinkedIn profile lists employers and titles. The MDB reader thinks in assignment rows, by kind of project and region (F4, F5). The site adds what the profile does not hold well:

1. **Selected work in the reader's own shape.** Rows of period, body, role, country or region, and one line of what he did and why it mattered. Sort by theme (PPP law, contract management, dispute resolution), not by employer only. This is the shape a staff member copies into a shortlist note or a direct-selection justification (World Bank 7.38–7.39).
2. **The work itself.** The 24 articles and talks in one place, each with a gateway page. A profile shows that he writes; the site lets the reader judge his judgement.
3. **A dated, current CV file** to attach to an internal note (F10).
4. **An address at his own domain** and one place where every fact agrees with LinkedIn. Stanford's first guideline is "Make it easy to verify". A date or title that differs from LinkedIn is a reason to doubt (inference).
5. **Do not repeat** the LinkedIn "About" text, skills lists or endorsements. The visitor has read them.

**How a reader feels qualifications instead of reading a list.** No study tests "felt" against "listed" credentials directly. The pattern below is an inference from F3–F6 and F9, and it matches Mark's voice rule 8.

- **Checkable specifics, not size words.** "a former Lead Lawyer in the World Bank's Infrastructure Practice Group" (his own article footnote) can be checked; a dated version can be checked more easily. "40 years of experience" cannot, and the voice guide bans it. The reader feels weight from named bodies, roles and dates.
- **Standing shown by who asked.** "The Asian Development Bank asked me to prepare …"; "FIDIC asked me to lead a Task Group …" (his own pattern, voice rule 8). This is the evidence that a direct selection needs: experience "of exceptional worth" (World Bank 7.39).
- **Work before claims.** Put the newest article or talk on the home page, with its venue. The reader meets his thinking before his CV (Edelman–LinkedIn, secondary).
- **A face in context.** A photo of him speaking at a named event gives social presence and standing at once (F9).
- **Once, in one place.** The profile states each credential once, as a plain fact. The rest of the site shows the same standing through work, not by repeating the credential.

**Downloadable or on-page CV.** Both, with different jobs. On-page: the selected-work rows and a short profile, to read and to feel. PDF: the full CV, dated, to take away. NN/g 2020 says a PDF is right only "where a printable PDF is needed". An MDB CV that goes into a file is that case.

## 4. Clashes and how each resolves

| Clash | Resolution |
|---|---|
| Show fees (NN/g) vs a bespoke-quote practice | The law draws the line (F8). Predetermined price: show it. Bespoke: say how the work is engaged and priced; give the method or an estimate on request. NN/g's "representative cases" suit packaged services, not MDB advisory work. |
| A face vs a firm that wants to look bigger | Show the real people. Simulated scale (a "we" for one person, a stock team) is invented proof, which the house bans (design-critique §2). A firm shows its lead and its real team. |
| Fewer fields vs qualifying the lead | The question protocol decides. The copy and the reply qualify; a field stays only when its answer changes the first reply. At most two optional fields. |
| Credential once, plainly vs make the reader feel the CV | State it once in the profile. The feel comes from specifics, from who asked him, and from the work. Do not repeat it or add adjectives. |
| A clear way to get in touch vs never end with "contact me" | The route is structure, not a sales line: the address as link text in every footer and at the end of the profile, and a "Contact" label in the navigation. Pages and articles end on his idea: most readers from a LinkedIn post are not buying yet, so the page builds memory, and the route must be findable when they are (F11). The voice guide contradicts itself here: rule 13 bans "get in touch", but its own hero draft keeps a "[Get in Touch]" button. Use the address or "Contact" instead. |
| Plain English (25 words) vs Mark's long sentences (mean about 30 words) | Split by copy type: task text and short copy plain; articles in his voice, with terms explained. |
| An on-page CV vs a PDF CV | Both: the page to read, the PDF to take. |
| A full geographic address (law) vs a home-based sole trader | The law wins where it applies; a service address can meet it (not checked for Mark). |
| "Selected work" rows that name the body vs voice rule 8: "never a named client or a mandate" | Rows name employers and the bodies that publicly asked or published his work (ADB, FIDIC, WAPPP). An assignment for a client is named only where Mark clears it (mmm-infra#10); otherwise the row names the kind of body and the region. |
| Third-party proof (NN/g factor 4) vs "invent none" | Link out to work that named bodies host. Show no testimonials until real ones exist with consent. |
| A phone number (NN/g minimum) vs an advisor who travels | Show it only if he answers it, labelled "Telephone:". Email alone is acceptable when it is clear and on every page. |

## Evidence log


### F1. NN/g on About Us, trust and B2B (read through the fetch tool)

- Kaley and Nielsen, "About Us Information on Websites", NN/g, 26 May 2019. Method: 3 rounds, expert reviews and qualitative tests, more than 70 users (business professionals included), 100 sites tested. Findings: users "liked to see photos of real employees"; stock photography hurt; "hard-to-find critical information (such as what the company does and whom to contact)" was a main cause of dissatisfaction; contact with "a diversity of options" was positive. The article gives no rule on bios or credentials (the 85 guidelines are in a paid report, not read). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/about-us-information-on-websites/ **Primary (the public article; the report not read).**
- Harley, "Trustworthiness in Web Design: 4 Credibility Factors", NN/g, 8 May 2016. Upfront disclosure: "prominently displaying contact information"; "stating any additional fees or charges". It does not name an About page. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/trustworthy-design/ **Primary.** Older than 2022; the factors are timeless.
- Fessenden, "What B2B Designers Can Learn from B2C About Building Trust", NN/g, 1 Sep 2019. "seeing a physical location and humans behind the product or service bolsters their trust"; "If you do have to request information from users, provide a reason why you are making the request"; "At a minimum, include an email address and phone number"; hiding prices makes a firm look "dubious"; buyers who want a long relationship read About and values pages; testimonial readers check the author's job title. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/b2b-trust-from-b2c/ **Primary** (no sample size given).
- Nielsen, "Show Prices for Common Scenarios", NN/g, 9 Apr 2006. A B2B site must "survive the pruning process and make it to the shortlist"; for custom work, "list representative cases and their prices". It does not say when to withhold price. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/show-prices-for-common-scenarios/ **Primary, 2006: flag as old.** The 2019 B2B-trust article repeats the rule, so it still holds for products and packaged services.
- NN/g "B2B Website Usability" report, 3rd edition, 188 guidelines, includes lead-generation forms. Paid; **not read.**

### F2. Reading level: GOV.UK, read at the source

- GOV.UK "Writing for GOV.UK" (GDS; published 25 Feb 2016, updated 13 Nov 2024), read in full as a 2024 Wayback capture through curl. Verbatim: "This is why we tell people to write on GOV.UK for a 9 year old reading age." The reason given is a reading skill (word-shape recognition by age 9), not a child's register. On specialists: "Research shows that higher literacy people prefer plain English"; "80% of people preferred sentences written in clear English - and the more complex the issue, the greater that preference"; "the more educated the person and the more specialist their knowledge, the greater their preference for plain English"; "Where you need to use technical terms, you can. They're not jargon. You just need to explain what they mean". Sentences: "check any sentences with more than 25 words to see if you can split them". **Primary.**
- The old URL now 301-redirects to guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk "Use clear language" (no date shown). That page keeps the 80% finding and "Try to split up sentences that are over 25 words long", but **its text does not state the age-9 target** (fetch tool, read). **Primary.**
- The 80% legal-language research is cited by GOV.UK without a link; its original study was not found or read.

### F3. How a development bank's borrower selects an individual consultant (World Bank)

- World Bank, Procurement Regulations for IPF Borrowers, 4th ed. (Nov 2020) and 7th ed. (Sep 2025), §7.34–7.39, read in full from the PDFs (thedocs.worldbank.org). The text is the same in both editions. **Primary; current.**
  - 7.34: an individual is hired where "the experience and qualifications of the individual are of paramount requirement".
  - 7.35: the individual chosen "shall be the most experienced and best qualified among the candidates"; "The evaluation shall be based on the relevant qualifications and experience of the individual Consultant."
  - 7.38 Limited Competitive Selection: "When the Borrower has knowledge of experienced and qualified individuals and their availability", it may invite them instead of advertising.
  - 7.39 Direct Selection, with justification: continuation of earlier work; "assignments with a total expected duration of less than six months"; urgent cases; or "relevant experience and qualifications of exceptional worth to the assignment".
- Consequence for a site: the buyer must judge *relevant* experience against a task (a TOR), compare candidates, and often must already *know of* the person (7.38) and *write a justification* (7.39). So a site serves two jobs: it makes the person known before a TOR exists, and it gives the staff member checkable facts to quote in a justification or an evaluation.
- These rules bind borrowers spending World Bank loans. They do not cover the Bank's own direct hiring of short-term consultants, which was not found in a public source.

### F4. How ADB selects an individual consultant

- Gillard (ADB Head of Consulting Services Unit), "Consultancy Recruitment – CSO Perspective", ADB Business Opportunities Seminar deck, 27 May 2026, read in full (pypdf text). Marked "INTERNAL ... may be shared outside ADB with appropriate permission", but posted on the public events.development.asia site. **Primary; current.**
  - Competitive selection of individuals: Terms of Reference, EOI evaluation criteria, "Review of CVs and EOIs", interviews optional. "direct awards may be made for most contracts <67days".
  - A CV is judged "as per the TOR": "work with the expert to emphasize (truthfully!) his/her relevant experience"; "Provide a summary: Total Years Experience in similar projects; Total Years Experience in similar geographic Area".
  - "Build reputation with ADB and in the industry" is one of six tips for winning work.
- ADB's Consultant Management System (cms.adb.org) is where individuals register a profile and submit EOIs (ADB "What is the Consultant Management System?", search snippet only). **Snippet-level.**
- Consequence: an MDB reader sorts experience by *similar project* and *similar region*, with years. A list of employers alone does not answer that. Short direct awards (under 67 days at ADB, under 6 months at World Bank borrowers) are where a known individual is picked without a contest.

### F5. What a development-bank CV form asks for

- Government of Belize, "Template for Application – Individual Consultant" for a World Bank-financed project (procurement.gov.bz, Aug 2025), read in full (docx text). **Primary for one borrower; the World Bank's own standard CV form (Form TECH-6 in its Standard RFP) was not reached.** The CV form asks for: "Employment record relevant to the assignment", reverse order, each row with "Period", "Employing organization and your title/position", "Country", and "Summary of activities performed relevant to the Assignment. Be specific."; contacts for references; skills "as stated in TOR"; languages; "Working experience in the region". The cover letter says selection "will be based on the professional background, qualification and experience of the applicants".
- Green Climate Fund and other RFPs use the same TECH-6 CV shape (education, memberships, countries worked in) (search snippets only).
- Consequence: the MDB reader's own mental template for "qualifications" is *assignment rows*: dates, client body, role, country, what was done.

### F6. How professional-services buyers "check out" a provider

- Hinge Research Institute, "Beyond Referrals: How Today's Buyers Check You Out", Feb 2014, read in full (PDF text). Survey of 1,028 buyers of professional services (accounting, legal, management consulting, A/E/C, among others). "Before you hire a professional services provider, how do you 'check them out'?": "Over 80% of buyers are looking to the web"; the seller's website is the most used resource; then online search; then asking colleagues; social media is used more than formal references; of social platforms, LinkedIn is the most used (70%). Buyers use 3.2 methods on average. **Primary, vendor-run survey (Hinge sells marketing), 2014: flag as old.** The direction (site checked before contact; LinkedIn the main social check) matches the 2024–2026 sources below and still holds; the percentages are dated.
- Hinge's later paid reports ("Inside the Buyer's Brain", 2nd ed., more than 1,400 buyers) were **not read**.
- Edelman–LinkedIn "B2B Thought Leadership Impact Report" 2024 (about 3,500 managers, 7 countries): "Nearly three-fourths of decision-makers say that thought-leadership content is more trustworthy for assessing an organisation's capabilities and competencies than its marketing materials." 2025 edition (about 2,000): 60% say thought leadership helps newer companies build credibility. Edelman and LinkedIn pages returned 403. **Secondary (PR-agency and trade-press recaps); vendor-commissioned survey.**

### F7. Enquiry-form length: what the evidence can and cannot carry

- NN/g, Whitenton, "Website Forms Usability: Top 10 Recommendations", 1 May 2016: "Keep it short"; "Every time you cut a field or question from a form, you increase its conversion rate"; "Limit the form to only 1 or 2 optional fields, and clearly label them as optional"; cut any field that can be derived, collected later, or left out. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/web-form-design/ **Primary; 2016, flag as old; the guideline is a heuristic from usability tests, not a measured field-count curve. Still current practice.**
- GOV.UK Service Manual, "Structuring forms" (last updated 7 Aug 2018): a "question protocol" "forces you (and your organisation) to question why you're asking users for each item of information"; ask a question only where "you need the information to deliver the service" and you know what you will do with it. https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/design/form-structure **Primary; timeless.** The NHS, HSE (Ireland) and Australian style manuals repeat it (search snippets).
- Field-count A/B tests are practitioner evidence only, and they point both ways: Marketo/MarketingExperiments (about 2011, snippet): a 5-field form converted at 13.4% vs 10% for 9 fields, but "collected less qualifying data". Aagaard (ContentVerve), CTAConf 2015 deck, read: 9 fields → 6 fields gave a "14.23% drop in leads" (512 conversions, 95% confidence); relabelling the 9 fields gave "19.21% increase". MarketingExperiments: a 15-field form beat an 11-field one (via Venture Harbour, 9 Apr 2026, which sells form software). HubSpot's 40,000-page and Eloqua's 1,500-page analyses (search snippets): conversions fall with more fields, but Eloqua's curve was flat to rising between 4 and 7. Zuko (vendor, via a third-party blog): a phone field has about 6.4% field abandonment. **All weak: single sites, vendor-run, unpublished data, conversion counted without lead quality.** No peer-reviewed field-count study of contact forms was found.
- Baymard's 8-field figure is for e-commerce checkout and does not transfer to an enquiry form (#104 W11).

### F8. What UK law makes a service provider show (where the provider is in the UK)

- Provision of Services Regulations 2009 (SI 2009/2999), reg. 8(1), read on legislation.gov.uk: name, "legal status and form"; geographic address and contact details "for rapid, direct communication"; trade-register name and number if registered; VAT number where VAT-liable; professional body and title in a regulated profession; professional liability insurance where required; and "Price, where the provider predetermines it for a given type of service". Reg. 8(2)(c): a website meets the duty ("easily accessible ... electronically"). Reg. 9(1): where the price is *not* predetermined, on request, "the method for calculating the price so that it can be checked by the recipient" "or a sufficiently detailed estimate". **Primary; current law.**
- Electronic Commerce (EC Directive) Regulations 2002 (SI 2002/2013), reg. 6(1), read: an information society service must make "easily, directly and permanently accessible" the provider's name, "geographic address", email address and details to contact "rapidly", register number, VAT number, and professional body and title where regulated. **Primary.** Whether a brochure site with no transactions is an "information society service" is commonly assumed but was **not checked** here (reg. 2 not read).
- UK GDPR Art. 13, ICO "When should we provide privacy information?" (no date; "under review"): where data is collected directly, e.g. on a form, privacy information is due "at the time when personal data are obtained"; "Simply putting it on your website, in case people happen to look there, is not enough." **Primary.** A `mailto:` link collects nothing on the site; a form does.

### F9. A face, and real people

- Nielsen, "Photos as Web Content", NN/g, 31 Oct 2010 (reviewed 13 Aug 2026). Eyetracking: on a team page, users looked 10% longer at the portraits than at the bios, though the bios took 316% more space; they are "real people who actually work at the company". Users "ignore stock photos of generic people". https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/ **Primary; 2010, but NN/g reviewed it in 2026; timeless.**
- Cyr, Head, Larios and Pan, "Exploring Human Images in Website Design", MIS Quarterly 33(3), 2009, 539–566: images with faces raised image appeal and perceived social presence, which raised trust; **no direct path** from human images to trust; images with people but no face scored worst; perception differed across Canada, Germany and Japan. **Secondary (abstracts and the SFU press note; the paper not read).** Timeless as a mechanism; the effect is indirect.
- Stanford Web Credibility guidelines (Fogg, 2002, read): "Show that honest and trustworthy people stand behind your site"; "Highlight the expertise ... Be sure to give their credentials"; "Show that there's a real organization behind your site ... listing a physical address"; "Make it easy to contact you". **Primary; 2002, flag as old; the four points recur in the 2016–2019 NN/g work, so they still hold.**
- Nielsen, "Deep Linking is Good Linking", NN/g, 2 Mar 2002: put "these three design elements on every single page": the company name or logo, a "Direct, one-click link to the homepage", and search. **Primary, 2002; timeless.**

### F10. A CV as a PDF, and the 24 article PDFs

- Nielsen and Kaley, "PDF: Still Unfit for Human Consumption, 20 Years Later", NN/g, 9 Aug 2020: "Do not use PDFs to present digital content that could and should otherwise be a web page"; offer a PDF only "in cases where a printable PDF is needed", through an HTML "gateway page" that summarises it; search should land on "the gateway page, never the PDF directly". https://www.nngroup.com/articles/pdf-unfit-for-human-consumption/ **Primary; 2020; current.**

### F11. The buyer is mostly not buying today

- Dawes (Ehrenberg-Bass) with the LinkedIn B2B Institute, the "95:5 rule", 2021: about 95% of B2B buyers are out of market at any time; Dawes says the figure is a heuristic, not exact. **Secondary (Marketing Week, mi-3, CustomerThink); the original note not read.**
- 6sense, "2024 Buyer Experience Report", 9 Oct 2024: 2,509 B2B buyers; buyers make first contact in more than 80% of cases, about 70% through their process, and about four in five have a preferred vendor before contact. **Snippet-level (vendor press release via search); mostly technology purchases.**
- Consequence: most visits from a LinkedIn post are memory-building, not a purchase. A site wins by being remembered and checkable when a TOR appears, which fits the voice guide's "never end with contact me". The contact route must still be findable at the moment the reader *is* in market (F1, NN/g 2019: hard-to-find contact was a main dissatisfaction).

### F12. Contact channel and response time

- GOV.UK A to Z style guide (current, read through curl): "Write email addresses in full, in lower case and as active links. Do not include any other words in the link text." Telephone: add "Telephone:" and group the number, for example "+44 (0)29 2018 0542". **Primary; current.** mmm-infra today: the hero button "Get in Touch" is a `mailto:` link whose text hides the address (site audit §2.3).
- Booking links (for example Calendly): **no source found** for or against on an advisor's site.
- A stated response time: **no independent study found.** The only figure (+28% enquiries) is from timetoreply, which sells the badge it tested. **Weak, vendor.** Research on fast replies (the HBR/MIT lead-response work, seen second-hand) is about the seller's follow-up, not the page's promise.
